## Purpose

The stage audit showed the ranking flips on pCTR error. This read asks
the follow-up: when two ads tie on estimated eCPM, who wins — and what
does the rule cost? Two deterministic scenarios place the same bidder
pair at an estimated-eCPM tie of 100.00; the estimate is conservative in
one and generous in the other. The tie-break rule (by bid, by quality
score) picks the winner, and true pCTR decides realized revenue.

## Output

```
tie-break read: two ads tie on estimated eCPM (100.00); the rule
decides who wins, and true pCTR decides what that slot earns

conservative estimate:
  Ad A: bid 2.00, est pCTR 0.05 (eCPM 100.00), true pCTR 0.05
  Ad X: bid 1.00, est pCTR 0.10 (eCPM 100.00), true pCTR 0.12
  tie-break by bid:          Ad A wins, realized 100.00
  tie-break by quality score:Ad X wins, realized 120.00

generous estimate:
  Ad A: bid 2.00, est pCTR 0.05 (eCPM 100.00), true pCTR 0.05
  Ad X: bid 1.00, est pCTR 0.10 (eCPM 100.00), true pCTR 0.08
  tie-break by bid:          Ad A wins, realized 100.00
  tie-break by quality score:Ad X wins, realized 80.00
```

## Notes

- Both rules are revenue-neutral under the estimate (100.00 either way);
  they diverge only under true pCTR. Quality-score tie-break realizes
  +20 when the estimate is conservative and -20 when it is generous.
- The by-bid rule's property is incentive: the advertiser's bid remains
  a price-taking statement, so bidding true value stays dominant
  (Varian, 2007). Tie-breaking by quality score makes the winner a
  function of the pCTR model, which invites gaming the estimate.
- Hand-built (bid, pCTR) pairs with no random draws; illustrative and
  deterministic. Real ties are broken by a policy rule chosen for its
  incentive properties, not by a single scenario's realized revenue.
